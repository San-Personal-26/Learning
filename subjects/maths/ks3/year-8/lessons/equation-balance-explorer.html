Explore: keeping the balance

What actually is an equation?

An equation is a statement that two amounts are exactly equal — like a set of scales that must stay level. Whatever's written on the left has to weigh the same as whatever's written on the right. When one side has a missing piece, written as x, "solving" the equation just means figuring out the one number that makes both sides balance.

The scales sit level — so both sides must be equal.

How do we solve it?

The rule that makes solving possible: whatever you do to one side, you must do to the other, or the scales tip and the statement stops being true. Add, subtract, multiply or divide both sides by the same amount, and the balance holds — which means you can strip away everything around x until it's alone on one side, with its value revealed on the other.

Where the word "algebra" comes from (a bit of history)

The word "algebra" comes from a 9th-century book called Al-Jabr, written by the Persian mathematician Al-Khwarizmi — the title roughly means "restoring broken parts," describing exactly the technique of moving a piece from one side of an equation to the other to keep it balanced. The equals sign itself is newer: it was invented in 1557 by the Welsh mathematician Robert Recorde, who chose two parallel lines because, in his words, "no two things can be more equal." Before that, mathematicians had to write out "is equal to" in words every single time.

Why equations matter beyond the classroom

Equations aren't just a maths-class exercise — they're how a recipe gets scaled up or down for a different number of guests, how a phone bill works out an unknown data allowance from a fixed monthly charge, how engineers calculate a missing measurement for a bridge or a shelf, and how a satnav works out a missing travel time from distance and speed. Anywhere one known relationship has to hold exactly, there's an equation underneath it.

Every one of today's equations comes down to one of two patterns. Here's what each one means, and where you'd actually see it.

B.7.1

One-step equations

If one operation is standing between x and being alone on one side, undo it by doing the inverse operation to both sides. Undo "+" with "−", undo "×" with "÷" — and whatever you do to one side of the balance, you must do to the other, or it stops being true.

A see-saw balances perfectly. One side holds a 14 kg sack of dog food. The other side holds a bag of cat food of unknown weight, x, plus a 6 kg sack of rice: x + 6 = 14. Subtract 6 kg from both sides of the balance to find the cat food's weight: x = 8 kg.

🔄 Inverse operations undo each other

Addition and subtraction are opposites — one undoes the other. So are multiplication and division. To isolate x, always apply the opposite of whatever is being done to it.

B.7.2

Two-step equations

Some equations have two operations to undo, like 2x + 3 = 11 — here x was multiplied by 2, then had 3 added. To solve it, undo the operations in reverse order: deal with the "+3" first, then the "×2".

A taxi charges a £3 flat fee plus £2 for every mile: 2x + 3 = 11 for an £11 fare. Subtract the £3 flat fee from both sides first (2x = 8), then divide both sides by the £2-per-mile rate (x = 4) — the journey was 4 miles.

🧦 Undo in reverse order

You put socks on, then shoes — so to undo it, you take shoes off first, then socks. An equation is built the same way: if x was multiplied then had something added, undo the "added" part first, then the "multiplied" part.
