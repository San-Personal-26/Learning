Explore: factors, multiples and primes

What actually is a factor?

A factor of a number divides into it exactly, with nothing left over. A multiple is what you get by counting up in that number — its own times-table. Every whole number has at least two factors: 1 and itself.

12 arranged as 3 rows of 4 — so 3 and 4 are both factors of 12.

How do we find them?

To check if one number is a factor of another, divide and see if it comes out exact — no remainder means it's a factor. To list multiples, just count up in that number: 5, 10, 15, 20... A number with more than two factors is called composite; a number with exactly two (1 and itself) is prime. Breaking a composite number all the way down into the primes that multiply together to make it is called prime factorisation.

A 2,200-year-old shortcut for finding primes (a bit of history)

Around 200 BCE, the Greek mathematician Eratosthenes devised a simple method for finding every prime number up to a limit: write out all the numbers, then repeatedly cross out the multiples of each prime you find, starting from 2. Whatever's left uncrossed is prime. It's called the Sieve of Eratosthenes, and it's exactly the idea behind the Prime Check tool below — try the "Run the Sieve" button there to watch it happen step by step. Around the same time, another Greek mathematician, Euclid, proved that there are infinitely many primes — a result still taught the same way today.

Why primes and factors matter beyond the classroom

Primes and factors aren't just a classroom idea — cicadas are the clearest example: some species spend exactly 13 or 17 years underground before emerging, and both are prime numbers. Scientists think that's deliberate, evolutionarily speaking — a prime-length cycle is much harder for a predator with a shorter cycle to sync up with, so prime-cycled cicadas get preyed on far less often. Gears use the same idea: a cog with a prime number of teeth wears more evenly, since each tooth meets every tooth on the gear it's turning against before any pattern repeats, instead of the same few teeth always grinding together.

Here's each idea in more detail — what it means, and where you'd actually use it.

A.3.1

Factors and multiples

A number's factors are every number that divides into it exactly. Its multiples are what you get multiplying it by 1, 2, 3, and so on. A number can have very few factors or quite a lot — it depends entirely on what divides into it.

A school hall needs 24 chairs arranged into equal rows with none left over. The possible row counts are exactly the factors of 24 — 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12 or 24 rows all work, but 5 rows would leave chairs over.

24 chairs as 4 rows of 6 — 4 and 6 are both factors of 24.

🔁 Every number has at least two factors

1 and the number itself always divide in exactly — even for a prime number, those are its only two factors.

A.3.2

Prime or composite?

A prime number has exactly two factors: 1 and itself. A composite number has more than two factors — it can be split into smaller equal groups in at least one other way.

17 sweets can only be shared out equally between 1 person (who gets all 17) or 17 people (who get 1 each) — there's no way to split them into equal smaller groups. That's exactly what makes 17 prime.

12 makes a neat rectangle (composite) — 13 can only ever form a single line (prime).

🎯 The only even prime

2 is the only even prime number — every other even number automatically has 2 as a factor, which means it's always composite.

A.3.3

Prime factorisation

Any composite number can be broken all the way down into a product of prime numbers — its prime factorisation. Keep splitting each part into two factors until every piece left is prime, and you can't split any further.

Splitting a stack of 60 building blocks into smaller and smaller equal piles — 60 into 2 piles of 30, then each 30 into 2 piles of 15, then each 15 into 3 piles of 5 — until nothing splits further is exactly what a factor tree does.

🌳 Order doesn't matter

Start splitting 12 as 2 × 6 or as 3 × 4 — either way, you end up with exactly the same primes: 2 × 2 × 3. There's only ever one prime factorisation for any number.
