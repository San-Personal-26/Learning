Explore: from a messy list to a clear picture

24 students, one question: "How do you get to school?"

Here are all 24 answers, exactly as they were collected — in the order students were asked, not sorted or counted yet. Try reading it and instantly saying which method is most popular. Hard, right? That's exactly the problem charts solve.

Categorical data
Data sorted into named groups rather than measured with numbers — like "Walk", "Car", "Bus" and "Bike" above.
Frequency
How many times something happens. "Walk" has a frequency of 9 because 9 students walk to school.
Frequency table
A table listing every category alongside its frequency — the first step in turning a messy list into something readable.
Class interval
A range used to group numerical data, like "10–19 minutes" — useful when there are too many different exact values to list separately.

Charts are surprisingly recent (a bit of history)

The bar chart and pie chart were both invented by the same person: William Playfair, a Scottish engineer, in the late 1700s and early 1800s. Before Playfair, numerical data was almost always presented in dense tables of numbers — exactly the kind of hard-to-read list this lesson opens with. Playfair drew the first statistical bar chart in 1786, in a book about trade between England and other countries, specifically because he believed "the eye is the best judge of proportion" and that a visual comparison would be immediate. He later invented the pie chart around 1801. Both inventions were controversial at the time — many scientists felt that serious data belonged in tables, not pictures. They came around.

Why charts matter beyond the classroom

Every graph in a news article, every chart in a government statistical release, every figure in a science paper uses one of the five types covered here. A frequency table turns a sprawling dataset into something a reader can scan in seconds; a bar chart makes a magnitude comparison instant; a pie chart shows a proportion at a glance. These aren't just classroom exercises — they are the standard toolkit for communicating numerical data to any audience, from a Year 8 class survey to a national census.

F.2.1

Frequency tables

Go through the raw list once, making a tally mark for each response, then add up the tallies per category. The result is a short table that says everything the long list said — just organised.

Bus5
Car7
Walk9

✏️ Why tally in groups of 5?

Tally marks are usually bundled in groups of five (four vertical strokes plus a diagonal fifth) so a long list can be counted in fives instead of ones — much faster to check, and much harder to lose your place.

F.2.2

Bar charts

Each category gets its own bar; the taller the bar, the higher the frequency. Bar charts make it easy to compare exact amounts at a glance.

🥧 Bar chart or pie chart?

Reach for a bar chart when you want to compare exact counts. Reach for a pie chart when you want to show how a total splits into parts — "about a third of the class walks" is a pie-chart kind of statement.

F.2.3

Pie charts

The whole circle represents everyone surveyed; each category gets a slice sized by its share of the total. Bigger slice, bigger share.

F.2.4

Pictograms

Instead of bars or slices, a pictogram uses repeated symbols. A key tells you what one symbol is worth — and a half-symbol can represent half that amount.

🧑🧑🧑

🧑 The half-symbol trick

If one symbol = 2 students, then an odd frequency like 9 needs 4 whole symbols and one half-symbol (4 × 2 + 1 = 9) — the half shows that "extra" 1 without needing a brand new symbol.

F.2.5

Grouping numerical data

Journey times like 4, 17, 22 and 33 minutes are all different — far too many exact values to give each its own bar. Instead, group them into equal-width class intervals (like 0–9, 10–19, 20–29, 30–39 minutes), then chart the frequency of each interval just like a bar chart.
