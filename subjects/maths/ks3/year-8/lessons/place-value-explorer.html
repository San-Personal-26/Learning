01

Explore: what is each digit worth?

What kind of numbers are we talking about?

Integers
Whole numbers — no fractions, no decimal point. 7, 250 and 1,000,000 are all integers. So is −3 (integers can be negative too).
Decimals
Numbers with a decimal point, for when a whole number isn't precise enough. "3.5 apples" doesn't really make sense, but 3.5 kilometres does — the decimal point lets us talk about the bit between whole numbers.
Measures
Numbers that describe a real quantity using a unit — how long, how heavy, how much liquid, how much time, how much money. Your height (1.62 m), the weight of an apple (150 g), the milk left in the bottle (1.5 l), how long break lasts (20 minutes), the price of a pencil (35p) — all measures. Notice they can be whole numbers or decimals, depending on how precise you need to be.

💡 Precision

A ruler can measure your height as 1.6 m, or more precisely as 1.62 m, or even 1.624 m. Each extra decimal place isn't a different number — it's the same height, just measured more precisely. A road sign says the distance is 12 km — nobody needs to know it's really 12.347 km to know when to turn off.

Spot the measure

A measure always has a unit attached, and it's describing "how much" of something. Sort these into the right pile.

0️⃣ The zero that isn't nothing

In 306, the zero looks like it's doing nothing — but take it out and you get 36, a completely different number. The zero holds the tens place empty so the 3 stays in the hundreds where it belongs. Same idea in 0.05: the zero after the point keeps the 5 in the hundredths place, not the tenths.

What happens when you ×10 or ÷10?

Multiplying or dividing by 10, 100 or 1000 doesn't change the digits themselves — it slides every digit sideways. Pick something real, then try it.

⚠️ Watch out

Which is bigger, 0.5 or 0.25? It's tempting to think 25 "looks bigger" than 5 — but 0.5 is a half, and 0.25 is a quarter, so 0.5 wins. More digits after the point doesn't mean a bigger number; it's still the place value that decides.

Recap

  • ✓Every digit's value depends on its place — the same digit can mean 8, 80, or 80,000 depending on where it sits.
  • ✓After the decimal point, places get smaller: tenths, then hundredths, then thousandths.
  • ✓Multiplying by 10, 100 or 1000 shifts digits left; dividing shifts them right.
  • ✓This shows up everywhere — prices, distances, race times, recipe quantities.
