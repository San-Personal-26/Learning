Explore: three rules about angles

What actually is an angle?

An angle is a measurement of turn. Picture two straight lines meeting at a single point, called the vertex. An angle tells you how far one line has rotated away from the other around that point — like a door swinging open from its hinge, or the hands of a clock spreading apart from 12. The bigger the angle, the further one line has turned relative to the other.

How do we measure it?

Angles are measured in degrees, written with a small ° symbol (e.g. 90°). A degree is a tiny slice of a full turn: if you spin all the way around in a circle back to where you started, that's 360°. So 90° is a quarter turn, 180° is a half turn (a straight line), and 360° is a complete turn back to the start. (Later in maths you'll meet another unit called radians — but degrees are what we'll use here.)

A quarter turn (shaded) is 90° — four of them make a full 360° turn.

Where does 360 come from? (a bit of history)

The choice of 360 isn't random, and it isn't modern — it goes back roughly 4,000 years to ancient Babylon. Babylonian mathematicians and astronomers used a counting system based on 60 (rather than our base-10), and 360 fits neatly into that system — it divides evenly by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12, and many more numbers, which made it very practical for the calculations they were doing by hand. It's also close to the number of days in a year, which the Babylonians were tracking closely for their calendar and astronomy — they watched the Sun appear to trace a full circle through the sky over the course of a year, and split that circular journey into 360 steps. The idea was passed on through Greek astronomers and mathematicians (including Hipparchus, who used it for early trigonometry and star-mapping) and has stuck ever since — every protractor, compass, and GPS system today still uses the Babylonians' 360°.

Why angles matter beyond the classroom

Angles aren't just an abstract maths topic — they're how ships and planes navigate (a bearing is an angle measured clockwise from North), how astronomers pinpoint stars and planets in the sky, how architects and engineers make sure buildings and bridges stand up safely, and how something as simple as a skateboard ramp or a bike gear is designed. Once you can reason about angles, you can describe direction, shape, and turning precisely — which is exactly what the three rules on this page let you do.

A ship's bearing of N 40° E — an angle measured from North.

Every one of today's angle problems comes down to one of three rules. Here's what each one means, why it's true, and where you'd actually see it.

D.10.1

Angles at a point

When two or more angles meet at a single point and go all the way around it back to where they started, they always add up to 360° — because a full turn, all the way around, is always 360°, no matter how you slice it up.

Think of a round pizza cut into slices from the centre. However unevenly you cut it — three big slices, six thin ones, whatever — the angles of every slice always add up to 360°, because together they make one full circle.

🔢 Why 360?

Long ago, Babylonian astronomers divided a full turn into 360 parts — probably because 360 divides evenly by so many numbers (2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10...). It stuck, and we still use it today.

D.10.2

Angles on a straight line

A straight line is itself a kind of angle — a flat one, worth exactly 180° (half a full turn). So if one or more angles sit along one side of a straight line, they always add up to 180° between them.

Prop a laptop lid open against a desk: the angle between the lid and the desk, plus the angle between the lid and the vertical, always adds up to 180° — because the desk and an imaginary line straight up from it together form one straight line.

📐 Right angle checkpoint

A straight line is 180°, so a right angle (90°) is exactly half of that — a handy benchmark. If an angle looks bigger than a right angle but smaller than a straight line, it's somewhere between 90° and 180°.

D.10.3

Vertically opposite angles

When two straight lines cross, they make four angles. The two angles directly opposite each other (across the crossing point, not next to each other) are called vertically opposite — and they're always exactly equal.

Open a pair of scissors, or picture an X-shaped road crossing: the angle at the top is always identical to the angle at the bottom, and the angle on the left is always identical to the angle on the right.

⚠️ Don't mix them up

Vertically opposite angles (across the X) are equal. The angles right next to them, along the same straight line, are not equal to them — they're supplementary, adding up to 180° instead.
