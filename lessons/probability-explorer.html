Explore: how likely is it?

You already talk about probability every day without realising it — "no chance," "I'll probably," "definitely." Maths just turns that everyday sense of "how likely" into a number, so it can be compared and calculated exactly.

E.1.1

Placing likelihood on a scale

Every probability is a number from 0 (impossible — it will never happen) to 1 (certain — it will definitely happen). Everything else sits somewhere in between: 0.5 is an even chance, below 0.5 is unlikely, above 0.5 is likely.

A weather forecast saying "70% chance of rain" is just this same scale wearing a percentage costume: 70% is 0.7, which sits a bit past "even chance," in "likely" territory.

☔ Percentages are probabilities too

0.7, 70%, and 7/10 are three ways of writing the exact same probability. If a forecast says 20% chance of snow, that's 0.2 on this same 0-to-1 scale — solidly "unlikely," but not impossible.

E.1.2

Equally likely vs unequally likely

Outcomes are equally likely when nothing makes one more probable than another — every face of a fair six-sided die has exactly the same 1-in-6 chance. Outcomes are unequally likely when something tips the balance — like a spinner where one section is much bigger than another.

Picture a spinner that's mostly blue with a small red wedge. You could still land on red — it's not impossible — but you'd land on blue far more often, simply because it takes up more of the circle.

⚖️ Fair doesn't always mean 50/50

A fair die has six equally likely outcomes, not two — "fair" means no outcome is favoured, not that there are only two options. Don't confuse "equally likely" with "even chance" (which specifically means probability 0.5).

E.1.3

Recording outcomes of an experiment

In probability, an experiment just means any action with an uncertain result — rolling a die, spinning a spinner, flipping a coin. Each time you do it, you get one outcome. Doing it many times and writing down what happens each time — in a frequency table — lets you see the pattern of results.

Flip a fair coin 20 times and tally the heads and tails. You'll rarely get exactly 10 and 10 — maybe 12 heads and 8 tails — and that's completely normal, not a sign the coin is unfair.

🎲 The coin has no memory

If a fair coin lands heads four times in a row, the next flip is still a 50/50 chance of heads — the coin doesn't "owe" you a tails. Each flip is independent; try it yourself in the playground below.

Recap

  • ✓Probability is a number from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain) — with unlikely, even chance and likely describing the space in between.
  • ✓Outcomes are equally likely when nothing favours one over another (a fair die); otherwise they're unequally likely (an uneven spinner).
  • ✓Recording the results of an experiment in a frequency table lets you compare what actually happened to what you expected.
  • ✓Random events have no memory — past results never change the probability of what happens next.
